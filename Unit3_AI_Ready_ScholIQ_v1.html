1
The Invented Expert
Hook & Discuss
Look at a real graded report — silently — then discuss what you notice.
10 min · Session 1 · Engage
Why This Activity?
Students enter the unit through a realistic, emotionally engaging scenario. The hook surfaces existing assumptions about AI reliability before any instruction begins. Show the image silently — give students 60 seconds to read it, then ask: “What do you notice?”
The Scenario — A Real Student Report
A student wrote a report with AI assistance. It received an A+. The teacher later discovered that the book listed — "The Future of Quantum Learning" (1927) — does not exist. Einstein never wrote it. AI invented it — with complete confidence — and neither the student nor the teacher caught it.
Discuss (one question at a time)
1. How would you tell the difference between a real piece of information and an invented one, if both look identical?
2. Is AI's confident tone enough proof that what it says is correct?
3. What is your responsibility as a user when you share information from AI with someone else?
Watch For…
Students who say “I always check AI” — probe: what does checking actually look like for them? Students who feel AI is always reliable — validate, then introduce the tension gently. Do not reveal answers yet — the goal is productive discomfort.
2
Hallucination Hunt
Explore
Ask AI about a niche topic, then verify every claim against a second, independent source.
15 min · Session 1 · Explore
Why This Activity?
Students directly experience AI hallucination rather than hearing about it abstractly. First-hand encounter creates lasting conceptual understanding and genuine motivation to verify. Groups that query very well-known topics see fewer hallucinations — redirect to niche or specific fact-based queries.
Step 1 — Ask AI about a niche topic (a historical figure, a local landmark, a scientific discovery)
Step 2 — Record the AI response
Step 3 — List the claims, verify each with a second source, then mark: ✅ Verified · ❓ Unverified · ❌ Incorrect/Invented
ReflectDid you find anything AI invented that sounded completely real? What does this tell you about using AI for research?
Watch For…
Groups that accept all AI output as correct — pause and ask them to run a single claim through a search engine. Clarify the difference: a hallucination is fabricated, not just mistaken. Introduce the term “Hallucination” during class debrief.
3
Why AI Hallucinates
Explain
Build a mental model: AI predicts language — not truth.
10 min · Session 1 · Explain
Why This Activity?
Students need a mental model of why hallucinations occur before they can develop systematic responses. Understanding the mechanism reduces fear and builds informed agency. Use the analogy of an “extremely well-read guesser.”
🔮
Predicts, Doesn't Look Up
AI generates text by predicting the most statistically likely next word — it does not "look things up" like a search engine does.
🧩
Fills Gaps Confidently
Trained to sound fluent and helpful, it may fill knowledge gaps with plausible-sounding content it has no actual evidence for.
🚫
No Uncertainty Alarm
AI does not know what it does not know — it has no built-in signal that says "I'm not sure about this."
The most important point in the unitAI predicts language — not truth. It does not verify facts before answering. Fluency is not accuracy.
Quick Poll — is AI hallucinating the same as AI lying?
Answer: No — AI is not intentionally deceptive; it lacks the awareness to know it is wrong.
Watch For…
Students who anthropomorphize AI (“It lied to me!”) — gently reframe: no intent, just pattern prediction. Students who conclude AI is useless — validate the frustration, pivot: “So what do skilled users do?”
4
Write a Verification Prompt
Build & Test
Return to your AI from Activity 2 and ask it to reflect on its own certainty.
15 min · Session 1 · Elaborate
Why This Activity?
Students practice an immediately applicable skill: turning AI into a self-checking partner. This activity closes Session 1 with agency — students leave with a tool they can use tomorrow.
Prompt Starters
A
"Look at the response you gave me above. For each claim, tell me: how confident are you, and how could I verify it?"
B
"Which parts of your previous answer might be incorrect or uncertain? Please flag them."
C
"Give me 3 reliable sources I can use to check what you just told me."
Which starter did you use, and how did AI respond?
Now write YOUR OWN original verification prompt (go beyond the starters)
ReflectDid AI's self-assessment match what you found in Activity 2? What surprised you?
Important LimitVerification prompts help, but AI can still be wrong about its own uncertainty. Always check with an independent source.
Watch For…
Students who copy the exact prompt starters — push for personalization: “What's a topic YOU care about verifying?” AI that resists the meta-prompt or gives vague self-assessments is a teachable moment: even the verification prompt has limits.

Warm-Up: Recall + Real Examples

8 min · Session 2 · Engage
Why This Activity?
Bridging from Session 1 reactivates prior learning and surfaces any remaining misconceptions before new content is introduced. Real-world examples ground the session in relevance.
Recall Prompts

6
Cross-Verification Lab
Explore & Apply
Apply the 4-step Cross-Verification Framework to a real AI Response Card, using the SOURCE test.
20 min · Session 2 · Explore
Why This Activity?
Students apply a structured method — not intuition — for evaluating AI output. The lab format makes verification feel like a skill, not a chore. Working in groups simulates real collaborative fact-checking.
The Cross-Verification Framework (CVF)
Identify Claims
Underline every factual claim in the AI response. What is the AI asserting as fact?
Source Hunt
Find at least 2 independent sources per claim. Who else says this, and are they credible?
Compare & Flag
Mark: ✅ Confirmed · ❓ Unverifiable · ❌ Contradicted. Does the evidence match the claim?
Summarize
Write a 2-sentence verdict on the AI response's reliability. Would you trust this response? Why / why not?
The SOURCE Credibility Test
Letter Question to Ask the Source
S · SectorWho published this? (.gov, .edu, established news org, or personal blog?)
O · ObjectiveIs the goal to inform, to sell, or to persuade?
U · UpdatedIs it recent enough to be relevant to this claim?
R · ReferencedDoes it cite its own sources or evidence?
C · ConsistentDo other credible sources say the same thing?
E · ExpertIs the author qualified to make this claim?
Rate each sourceHigh (H) · Medium (M) · Low (L) credibility. This rating becomes part of your Fact-Check Report.
Topic of your AI Response Card & claims identified
Verification Log — claim, source used, credibility (H/M/L), verdict (✅❓❌)
Watch For…
Groups that rely on a single Google search as “verification” — apply the SOURCE test together. Groups that verify only easy claims — guide them to flag harder-to-check assertions too. Groups that rate all sources “High” reflexively — push them to apply each SOURCE letter explicitly.
7
Fact vs. Opinion vs. Invention
Explain & Sort
Learn to categorize AI claims, then sort 8 AI-generated sentences.
10 min · Session 2 · Explain
Why This Activity?
Many students conflate these three categories. Making the distinction explicit equips them to evaluate not just accuracy but also the nature of AI claims — a meta-cognitive leap.
FACT
A verifiable claim that can be confirmed or refuted. Clue words: "was born," "discovered," "costs," "studies show."
OPINION
A judgment or interpretation — reasonable people may disagree. Clue words: "is considered," "many believe," "arguably."
INVENTION
A fabricated claim presented as fact — cannot be verified because it does not exist. Details sound specific but trace to nothing.
Sorting Exercise — Label each sentence: F / O / I
# AI-Generated Sentence Your Label
1"Marie Curie was born in Warsaw in 1867."
2"Curie is widely considered the greatest scientist of the 20th century."
3"In 1903 Curie published The Science of Radiance, her most influential book."
4"Radioactivity was first observed by Henri Becquerel in 1896."
5"Einstein's 1919 paper on quantum education changed school systems worldwide."
6"Many experts argue that Curie's work is underrepresented in school curricula."
7"The Curies received the Nobel Prize together in 1903."
8"Science education has benefited enormously from Curie's legacy."
NoteSentences 3 and 5 contain invented information — the books/papers named do not exist. Compare your answers with your group.
Watch For…
Students who label all AI output as “fact” — remind: AI does not label its own claims; the reader must categorize. Disagreement on Opinion vs. Fact is productive; use it to deepen discussion about subjectivity.
7B
When AI Disagrees With Itself — Spotting Bias
Explain
Compare two AI tools' answers to the same debatable question — and ask why they differ.
7 min · Session 2 · Explain
Why This Activity?
Bias appears in the unit concepts but had no learning activity. This short activity makes it visible, experiential, and directly tied to the unit's critical thinking goals. Students discover that the same question can produce meaningfully different AI answers — and ask why.
Same Question, Two AI Tools
"Is social media beneficial for teenagers?"
Response A
"Research consistently shows that social media strengthens social bonds, supports mental health through peer connection, and gives young people a voice in public discourse. Used responsibly, it is a net positive for adolescent development."
Response B
"Studies indicate that heavy social media use is linked to increased anxiety, sleep disruption, and social comparison in teenagers. While some benefits exist, the risks to adolescent wellbeing are significant and well-documented."
Both responses sound confident and factual. Which one is correct?
What might explain why two AI tools gave such different answers on the same topic?
What does this tell you about trusting a single AI response on a debatable topic?
Key ConceptAI bias can come from training data, prompt wording, or system design. It often looks like a reasonable, confident answer, not an obvious error — that is what makes it worth checking. For contested topics especially, always seek more than one source and more than one AI tool.
Watch For…
Students who conclude “AI is always biased and useless” — reframe: bias exists on a spectrum; the skill is recognizing and accounting for it. Students who pick one response as simply “more true” — push: on genuinely debatable topics, the answer is not always binary.
8
My Fact-Check Report
Elaborate · Summative
Select a topic, generate an AI response, apply the CVF, and produce a written Fact-Check Report.
15 min · Session 2 · Elaborate
Why This Activity?
This is the summative performance task of Unit 3. Students synthesize all unit skills into a concrete, transferable product. The report format mirrors real-world fact-checking practice. Share the Rubric (D4) before students begin.
Independently select a topic, generate an AI response, and apply everything you learned today. This is a real product you can stand behind.
Topic & AI Query — state the topic and the exact prompt used
AI Response Summary — paraphrase the AI's response (do not copy)
Verification Log — at least 4 claims: source, credibility (H/M/L), verdict (✅❓❌)
Reliability Verdict — rate the AI response (High/Medium/Low) and justify
Verification Decision — would you share this response with others as-is? What would you change first?
Reflection — what will you do differently next time you use AI for research or publishing?
Watch For…
Circulate — check students flag at least one ❓ or ❌ claim. Students who only verify “safe” obvious facts — redirect: the value is in checking what seems credible but might not be. Students who use AI to verify AI must be flagged directly: “Can a source verify itself?”

Lesson Flow Notes
Timing and facilitation tips across both sessions.
Session 1 · 0–10 min · Activity 1
Show the Invented Expert image silently on the projector for 60 seconds. Ask "What do you notice?" before revealing context. Do not reveal answers yet — the goal is productive discomfort.
Session 1 · 10–25 min · Activity 2
Groups of 3–4, one AI-accessible device each. Redirect well-known-topic queries toward niche or specific fact-based ones — hallucinations are rarer on famous topics. Record findings in a class "Real vs. Invented" column.
Session 1 · 25–35 min · Activity 3
Use the "extremely well-read guesser" analogy. Emphasize the key distinction: a search engine retrieves existing documents; AI generates new text based on patterns — it can invent the thing itself.
Session 1 · 35–50 min · Activity 4 + Exit
Push for personalized prompts, not copied starters. Distribute Exit Ticket S1 (D1) for the last 5–10 minutes; sort into Clear/Partial/Needs re-teaching before Session 2.
Session 2 · 0–8 min · Activity 5
Open with the most common gap revealed by Exit Ticket S1. Share 1–2 real published hallucination examples (fabricated legal citations, invented historical events).
Session 2 · 8–28 min · Activity 6
Teach the SOURCE test explicitly before Step 2 begins (5 min). Push groups that rate all sources "High" reflexively to apply each SOURCE letter explicitly.
Session 2 · 28–38 min · Activity 7
Project or print the 8 AI-generated sentences from Package B, Page 3. Sentences 3 and 5 contain invented information — use disagreements on Opinion vs. Fact to deepen discussion.
Session 2 · 38–45 min · Activity 7B
Display Response A and B side-by-side. If discussion goes flat, switch the topic to something students care about (homework, video games).
Session 2 · 38–53 min · Activity 8
Circulate — confirm every student flags at least one ❓ or ❌ claim. Flag directly any student using AI to verify AI: "Can a source verify itself?"
Session 2 · 53–60 min · Exit + Closing
7 minutes for Exit Ticket S2 (D2). Run the three-step closure: Headline, Commitment Card, Bridge to Unit 4.
