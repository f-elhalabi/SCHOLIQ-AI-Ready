The Shortcut Trap
Engage & Discuss
Read Nour's scenario, then answer the discussion questions honestly.
10 min · Session 1 · Engage
Why This Activity?
Students arrive with existing shortcut habits. Before teaching better use, we surface the habit and examine it without shame. A scenario-first opening respects the curriculum's principle: every lesson starts with a real situation, never a definition.
The Scenario
Nour has a biology test tomorrow on cell division. She opens an AI tool and types: "Explain cell division." AI gives her a clear, detailed explanation. She reads it once. She feels ready. The next morning, she fails the test.
Discuss with a Partner (90 seconds)
1. What did Nour do right?
2. What did she do wrong?
3. What should she have done differently?
Watch For…
Students may say “Nour was lazy” — redirect: “Let's focus on the method, not the person.” Avoid naming specific AI tools during this discussion.
2
Prompt Anatomy Workshop
Compare
Compare three versions of the same prompt, then write your own weak and strong prompt.
15 min · Session 1 · Explore
Why This Activity?
Before students can use AI as a learning tool, they need a shared language for talking about prompts. The “bad → better → best” structure uses comparison as a learning strategy — more effective than examples alone.
❌
Weak
"Explain photosynthesis." — Too vague, AI doesn't know your level or purpose.
⚠️
Better
"Explain photosynthesis in simple terms." — Slightly better, still no role or context.
✅
Strong
"You are a science teacher for a 9th-grade student. Explain photosynthesis step-by-step, using an everyday analogy. I have already studied plant cells." — Role + Task + Context.
Write Your Own — pick a topic from any class you have right now
My WEAK prompt (one vague sentence)
My STRONG prompt (Role + Task + Context)
Watch For…
Common mistake: students write “You are a helpful assistant” as the role — push for subject-specific roles: “You are a history teacher,” “You are a math tutor.”

The 3-Part Formula
Explain & Demo
Name the three parts of every strong learning prompt, then watch a live demo.
10 minutes — Session 1 — Explain
Why This Activity?
After students have struggled with prompts themselves (Explore), they are ready to receive the structured framework — explore before explain, following constructivist principles. The live demo is critical: students need to see the formula produce a better result in real time.

Live Demo
Type a weak prompt first on a topic students are currently studying, then the 3-Part version. Let students observe the difference in depth, clarity, and usefulness.

Watch For…
If your AI tool gives an impressive response to the weak prompt too — acknowledge it: “Yes, AI is getting better at guessing. But you are in control — don't rely on AI to guess what you need.”
4
Upgrade My Prompt
Build & Test
Build a real prompt for a topic you are studying right now, test it with AI, then apply the Verify-Reflect Cycle.
15 min · Session 1 · Elaborate
Why This Activity?
Practice must be generative, not imitative. Students write prompts connected to their actual schoolwork — this creates immediate transfer. The “test it” step is essential: students must see AI's response and evaluate it.
Step 1 — Pick a topic from any current class
Step 2 — Write a weak prompt (1 line)
Step 3 — Upgrade using the 3-Part Formula
Step 4 — Test it with AI. Summarize the response (do not copy-paste)
Step 5 — Verify-Reflect Cycle
Watch For…
Alert: if students start using AI for other homework during this activity — pause the class and redirect: “We are learning how to use AI, not using it to do work right now.”

6
AI Learning Stations
Rotate & Apply
Use a fresh 3-Part prompt at each station. After AI responds, complete the output task — don't skip it.
20 min · Session 2 · Explore
Why This Activity?
Stations allow differentiated, active engagement across four academic contexts. Rotation builds fluency: writing one strong prompt is a skill; writing four in four contexts is internalization. The verification step prevents passive acceptance of AI output.
🔵 Station 1 — History Event
Starter: You are a history teacher for a [grade] student. Explain the causes of [event] step-by-step. I already know the basic events but not why they connect.
Output: 3 connecting ideas in your own words
🟢 Station 2 — Math Concept
Starter: You are a math teacher. I'm struggling with [concept]. Walk me through it step-by-step with a worked example, then give me one problem to solve myself.
Output: explain in your words, then solve (show work)
🟡 Station 3 — Science Process
Starter: You are a science teacher. Explain [process] using an everyday analogy. I'm a [grade] student. I already know [...].
Output: evaluate the analogy — what's good, what's missed?
🔴 Station 4 — Writing Feedback
Starter: I wrote this paragraph: [paste]. Give me 3 specific suggestions to improve argument or clarity. Do NOT rewrite it for me.
Output: apply one suggestion, rewrite the sentence, explain why
Watch For…
The verification step built into each station prevents the most common misuse — accepting AI output without thinking. Rotate every 5 minutes, or run simultaneously if enough devices.
7
Group Share-Out
Explain
Share your best prompt today, and what you did with the AI response that showed you were thinking.
10 min · Session 2 · Explain
Why This Activity?
After individual and pair work, whole-class synthesis consolidates learning and surfaces diverse examples. This also serves as informal assessment — you hear what students actually understood, not just what they wrote.
What was the best prompt you wrote today, and what showed you were thinking?
Watch For…
If a group cannot identify a moment of thinking — ask: “Did the AI ever say something you weren't sure about? What did you do then?” Use a “one new voice per round” rule.
8
My Learning Summary
Signature Product
This is Unit 2's signature artifact — proof you used AI as a thinking partner, not a content provider.
15 min · Session 2 · Elaborate
Why This Activity?
The explicit instruction “use AI to help you understand — not to write this for you” is pedagogically critical. This task embeds metacognition: students must reflect on the process, not just produce a product.
AI helps you understand; but this summary is written entirely in YOUR words. No copy-paste. No paraphrasing AI sentences.
My topic
What I Learned — 3–5 sentences, in your own words
How AI Helped — the prompt you used and HOW it helped your thinking
What I Still Wonder — the most important part
A student who used AI well comes out with better questions, not fewer. Write 1–2 questions that came up while using AI today.
Watch For…
Most common failure mode: Part 1 paraphrases AI, Part 2 says “AI helped me understand the topic” with no specifics. Push for specificity in both. Part 3 often produces the richest learning conversations.

Teacher Guide · Unit 2 · Package A
Lesson Flow Notes
Timing and facilitation tips across both sessions.
Session 1 · 0–10 min · Activity 1
Ask the show-of-hands question, no comment. Read Nour's scenario aloud. 90 seconds pair discussion, then 3–4 responses on the board.
Session 1 · 10–25 min · Activity 2
Write the 3 prompt versions on the board. Circulate — push for subject-specific roles and real context, not generic phrasing.
Session 1 · 25–35 min · Activity 3
Live demo is critical — type a weak prompt, then the 3-Part version, on a topic students currently study. Let them observe the difference.
Session 1 · 35–50 min · Activity 4 + Exit
Circulate actively during testing. Watch for students drifting to other homework — redirect immediately. Collect Exit Ticket 1 before dismissal.
Session 2 · 0–8 min · Activity 5
Cold-call 3 students for the formula. If most cannot recall it, spend 3 extra minutes re-teaching before moving on.
Session 2 · 8–28 min · Activity 6
Rotate stations every 5 minutes, or run simultaneously with enough devices. The verification step at each station is not optional.
Session 2 · 28–38 min · Activity 7
Use a "one new voice per round" rule. Listen for evidence of verification, building on AI, or noticing errors.
Session 2 · 38–53 min · Activity 8
Check Part 3 (Still Wonder) specifically while circulating. If empty, sit with the student and ask: "What's one thing AI said you're not 100% sure about?"
Session 2 · 53–60 min · Exit + Closing
7 minutes for Exit Ticket 2. Return to the Essential Question — 30 seconds silence before asking for changed answers. Bridge briefly to Unit 3.

Differentiation by Cognitive Level
Every activity in this unit can be adjusted to meet students at three levels — not a rigid prescription.
5.1 · Grade-Band Adaptation Matrix
Grades 7–8

Grades 11–12
Evaluate & Create
Design → Critique → Transfer
Activity 🟦 Grades 7–8 🟪 Grades 9–10 🟩 Grades 11–12
A1 · The Shortcut Trap Name what Nour did in one verb — do not open the debate yet. Name the step Nour skipped; predict her result and give the reason. Is the problem Nour, or the way we assess learning?
A2 · Prompt Anatomy Workshop Label which prompt is weak/medium/strong; underline what makes the strong one strong. Write the strong version themselves, then compare with the model. Write a deliberately weak prompt that still looks plausible — explain why plausibility is the real danger.
A3 · The 3-Part Formula Copy the Role/Task/Context structure using their own subject, from a worked example. List three specific differences the live demo produced — not "better," but what changed. Critique the formula: propose and defend a fourth part it is missing.
A4 · Upgrade My Prompt Use the sentence stem; one prompt, one revision. Write their own 3-part prompt, run it, revise once, log the change. Write a multi-turn sequence — opening prompt plus two follow-ups that build on each response.
A6 · AI Learning Stations Two stations, teacher-modelled; verify one fact against the textbook. All four stations; apply the Verify–Reflect Cycle at each. Choose a topic where AI is likely to err; document the error and the prompt fix.
A8 · Learning Summary Parts 1–2 only; a drawing may replace writing. All three parts, with one specific example of AI changing their thinking. Add Part 4 — the next question to ask — and name a subject where this method would fail.
No-Device / Low-Tech Fallback — All Grade BandsThe teacher models every AI interaction on the projector; students write their prompts on paper and predict the response before it is revealed. Prediction-first raises metacognitive demand rather than lowering it.
Grades 11–12 without devicesRun A6 as a whole-class adversarial exercise — the class writes a prompt designed to make the projected model fail, then analyses the result together.
5.2 · Within-Band Support Levels
